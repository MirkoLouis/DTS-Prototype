# **Review of Related Literature and Studies**

This chapter provides a comprehensive review of the theoretical foundations, scholarly literature, and existing technical systems that inform the development of the Document Tracking System (DTS) prototype. The review explores the global and local challenges of administrative transparency, the technical evolution of cryptographic integrity, and the socio-technical implications of informal "shadow systems" in the public sector. By synthesizing these diverse perspectives, the chapter establishes the necessity of moving from policy-based security to mathematical-based assurance within government workflows.

The chapter is structured into five main sections. Section 2.1 (Related Literature) employs a thematic approach to discuss the conceptual foundations of document management, cryptographic security, and physical-digital synchronization. Section 2.2 (Related Studies/Systems) compares existing foreign and local applications, highlighting their technical strengths and operational gaps. Section 2.3 (Synthesis of the Review) identifies the specific research voids that this study aims to fill. Finally, Sections 2.4 and 2.5 present the Theoretical and Conceptual Frameworks that guide the design and evaluation of the DTS artifact.

## **2.1 Related Literature**

To establish a rigorous foundation for this study, the related literature is organized into six thematic pillars: administrative inefficiencies, cryptographic integrity, physical-digital synchronization, shadow IT workflows, system scalability, and user-centric design frameworks.

### **2.1.1 Administrative Bottlenecks and the Failure of Manual Systems**

The transition from physical to digital administration is driven by the persistent failure of manual methods to handle modern correspondence volumes. Manual document tracking represents the traditional "pen-and-paper" era of bureaucracy, where the movement of files is recorded in physical logbooks and routing slips. While this method was historically sufficient for low-traffic environments, it is increasingly viewed as an obstacle to modern governance due to its lack of searchability, physical vulnerability, and systemic opacity (Bala, 2020; Muhammad, 2020).

A synthesis of global and local literature reveals a consensus on the severe consequences of these manual bottlenecks. Researchers like Rokosu (2025) and Piloton (2023) have quantified these failures, reporting that manual systems in local government units suffer from a 40% document misplacement rate and prolonged delays that average three to six weeks. Furthermore, Acoba (2024) and Maina et al. (2025) highlight that even in the Philippine judicial and administrative sectors, the reliance on official record books leads to "information silos" where accountability is impossible to enforce. While some studies focus on the technical loss of data (Nihare et al., 2020; Kolte, 2021), others emphasize the legal contradictions between manual slow-downs and the strict turnaround times mandated by Republic Act No. 11032 (Acedo, 2025; Piloton, 2023).

These literatures help this research by providing the empirical justification for a centralized digital tracking solution. By documenting the specific failure points of physical logbooks—such as lost tracking codes and the "ghost document" phenomenon—these studies allow the researchers to design a system that specifically targets institutional bottlenecks. The literature establishes that a successful DTS must go beyond simple digitization; it must implement mandatory intake protocols to prevent the operational bypassing that occurs in the manual workflows described by Agarkar et al. (2012) and Akçay et al. (2019).

### **2.1.2 Cryptographic Integrity and Tamper-Evident Architectures**

The evolution of document security has shifted from traditional policy-based access levels toward mathematical-based assurance. In standard information systems, security is often "policy-based," meaning it relies on the hope that administrators will not abuse their access to change historical records. However, modern cryptographic literature argues that for government data, security must be built directly into the data structure itself to ensure that the history of a document remains immutable and verifiable (Bajoria, 2025; Crosby & Wallach, 2009).

Synthesis of current research shows that tamper-evident logging and digital signatures are the foundational technologies for this shift. Chavarro et al. (2018) and Crosby and Wallach (2009) highlight the importance of "history trees" and hash-chaining, where each record is mathematically linked to its predecessor. This concept is further strengthened by the work of Bernstein et al. (2012) on Ed25519 signatures, which provides high-speed, non-repudiable proof of action. While recent foreign studies focus on the decentralization of these chains via blockchain (Mishra & Sato, 2025; Zhai et al., 2022), there is a growing debate on how to implement such high-integrity ledgers within centralized databases optimized for local government hardware (Bajoria, 2025; Kim & Kim, 2024).

These literatures are vital to the development of the DTS "Trust Builder" module. They provide the mathematical frameworks—specifically SHA-256 hash-chaining and Ed25519 elliptic curve signatures—needed to create an immutable audit trail. By building upon these concepts, the researchers can ensure that the prototype provides a level of security that exceeds the capabilities of traditional government systems. The literature confirms that by shifting trust from human supervisors to mathematical proofs (Singh, 2024; Zhai et al., 2023), the system can effectively eliminate "silent tampering" and ensure institutional accountability.

### **2.1.3 Physical-Digital Synchronization via QR and IoT Technologies**

Effective document tracking requires a seamless bridge between the physical movement of files and their digital records. Synchronization technology ensures that the status displayed on a computer screen accurately reflects the "ground truth" of a document's physical location. Without this link, a tracking system becomes a mere repository of disconnected data, failing to provide the real-time visibility needed for administrative oversight (Acedo, 2025; Farin, 2024).

The synthesis of literature on synchronization identifies QR codes as the most practical and acceptable technology for public sector environments. Farin (2024) and Acedo (2025) found that QR scanning is highly accepted in Philippine institutions due to its cost-effectiveness and ease of use. While more advanced technologies like NFC and IoT (Abidin et al., 2019, 2020) offer superior real-time localization, they are often seen as too expensive or complex for local government units. Conversely, studies on barcodes (Rellon et al., 2020) and watermarking (Rimando & Corpuz, 2022) suggest that while functional, they lack the rapid scanning and identification capabilities of modern QR tags (Kwon et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2023).

This literature helps the study by justifying the selection of QR code technology as the primary mechanism for department-to-department hand-offs. It establishes that for a system to be "active," it must require a physical scan to move a document from an "in transit" to a "processing" state. This synchronization directly supports the requirements of RA 11032 by providing verifiable, timestamped proof of every transfer. Furthermore, by learning from the acceptability studies of Jaquilmo (2023) and Hermosa (2023), the researchers can design a scanning interface that minimizes administrative friction while maximizing physical accountability.

### **2.1.4 Shadow IT and the Evolution of Unofficial Workflows**

The proliferation of "Shadow IT"—unofficial software solutions deployed by staff to bypass failing official systems—represents a significant cybersecurity and data integrity risk. Shadow systems usually emerge as a rational coping mechanism when formal IT solutions are perceived as too rigid, slow, or disconnected from actual workflows (Behrens, 2009; Rentrop & Zimmermann, 2020). In many government offices, this manifests as a reliance on unencrypted Google Sheets and Forms to manage critical document metadata (Muhammad, 2019; NinjaOne, 2022).

A synthesis of the literature identifies a critical tension between operational speed and official security. Behrens (2009) and Rentrop and Zimmermann (2020) argue that Shadow IT provides a "human-centric" flexibility that official systems often lack. However, Haag and Eckhardt (2024) and Marchewitz (2026) warn that these unapproved systems lead to fragmented databases and severe vulnerabilities, as they lack the ACID compliance and mathematical security needed for government records. This trend is worsening in the AI era, where staff can easily deploy consumer-grade tools that are faster than institutional software but significantly less secure (Marchewitz, 2026; NinjaOne, 2022).

This literature helps the research by framing the DTS prototype not just as a security tool, but as a "bespoke" alternative to shadow systems. By recognizing that staff use Google Sheets for its speed and convenience (Haag & Eckhardt, 2024; Towards a Bespoke DTS, 2019), the researchers can focus on developing features—such as smart routing and sub-second load times—that compete with the perceived benefits of informal tools. The literature informs the design philosophy of the project: to eliminate shadow IT, the official system must be more usable, more reliable, and faster than the informal alternatives it seeks to replace.

### **2.1.5 Performance Optimization and Scalability in High-Volume Systems**

Systems designed for large government divisions like DepEd must be capable of handling millions of records without a degradation in performance. For a Document Tracking System, scalability is not just about storage; it is about the system's ability to perform complex cryptographic audits and search queries in sub-second response times (FormKiQ, 2023; SunSmart Global, 2022). High-volume environments create significant stress on centralized databases, requiring advanced architectural patterns to maintain institutional efficiency.

The synthesis of literature on system performance highlights a shift toward asynchronous processing and isolated data structures. FormKiQ (2023) and SunSmart Global (2022) emphasize that traditional server-based models must incorporate background jobs to handle intensive tasks like report generation. More importantly, Kim and Kim (2024) introduced a structured hash chain approach that isolates logs into document-specific chains. This "micro-sharding" technique ensures that the mathematical overhead of hash calculation does not slow down the entire system as the record volume grows. This technical consensus is supported by various engineering studies on the use of iterative models and modern frameworks (Aqeel et al., 2023; Izzulhaq et al., 2022; Lee & Paik, 2019; Rosidin et al., 2023).

These literatures help the study by providing the technical blueprint for the DTS prototype's performance layer. By incorporating asynchronous background processing (Thohir et al., 2022) and efficient search indexing (Krohn, 2019), the researchers can ensure the system remains responsive under heavy load. The work of Kim and Kim (2024) specifically informs the "micro-sharding" of the hash chain, allowing the system to verify individual document integrity without needing to scan the entire global ledger. This ensures that the system meets the high-volume performance metrics defined in Chapter 3 while maintaining strict mathematical security.

### **2.1.6 Human-Centric Design and Technology Acceptance**

The ultimate success of a technological artifact is determined by its acceptance and adoption by its human users. Even the most secure system will fail if it is perceived as too complex, culturally insensitive, or disconnected from organizational habits. Therefore, the design of a Document Tracking System must be grounded in theories that explain how technology integrates with human behavior and decision-making processes (Bertalanffy, 1968; Frankel et al., 2018).

A synthesis of human-centric literature reveals that technology acceptance is driven by perceived usefulness and the reduction of systemic uncertainty. Frankel et al. (2018) and Bertalanffy (1968) provide the theoretical foundations for viewing the office as a socio-technical system where technological tools must support, rather than disrupt, established habits. Studies by Rokayah et al. (2022) and Takahashi et al. (2022) demonstrate that systemic stressors—such as information gaps or complex protocols—can lead to hesitancy and resistance. Conversely, providing clear, data-driven features through Decision Support Systems (Keen & Scott Morton, 1978; Ngan et al., 2022; Gonzalez et al., 2019) has been shown to improve engagement and trust (Christinna & Saragih, 2022; Khairi et al., 2022; Multidisiplin et al., 2025).

This literature helps the research by guiding the UI/UX design and the implementation of "smart" features. By utilizing Decision Support Theory, the researchers integrated an AI-assisted route prediction module to help Records Officers manage complex routing decisions. The literature on technology acceptance ensures that the cryptographic actions—such as PIN-based signing—are presented in a familiar, non-intimidating way. By prioritizing the "human factor" and institutional trust, the study aims to create a system that is not only mathematically secure but also culturally and operationally acceptable to the personnel of DepEd Iligan City.

## **2.2 Related Studies/Systems**

This section evaluates applied research and existing document tracking systems, distinguishing between foreign architectural innovations and local implementations tailored to the Philippine administrative context.

### **2.2.1 Foreign Studies and Systems**

The global landscape of document tracking has shifted toward high-integrity architectures that integrate cryptographic ledgers within centralized database environments. Foreign studies increasingly address the limitations of traditional policy-based security by proposing "Mathematical-Based" alternatives that satisfy strict administrative and financial compliance rules. These studies serve as the technical vanguard for modernizing e-governance infrastructure through the use of immutable ledgers and automated verification (Bajoria, 2025; Kim & Kim, 2024).

A synthesis of international research identifies a clear trend toward scalable, process-specific security models. Kim and Kim (2024) demonstrated that adaptive hash chains can provide real-time auditability without the computational bottlenecks typically associated with global blockchains. This is complemented by the work of Bajoria (2025), who proved that blockchain-inspired audit trails can be successfully implemented within standard Relational Database Management Systems (RDBMS). Furthermore, the long-term success of the U.S. Federal Courts' CM/ECF system (Greenwood, 2012) and the digital transformation of the Malaysian (Saman & Haider, 2012) and Indonesian (Electronic Judiciary in Indonesia, 2021) judiciaries underscore the importance of centralized repositories in achieving paperless transactions and immediate document access. However, as noted by Zhai et al. (2022) and Al-khafaji et al. (2020), these repositories require advanced authority authentication to ensure the authenticity and objectivity of historical data.

These foreign studies help this thesis by establishing the architectural benchmarks for the "Trust Builder" module. The work of Kim and Kim (2024) specifically informs the "micro-sharding" design of the DTS prototype, allowing for fast verification even in high-volume environments. By adopting the principles of high-integrity centralized ledgers proposed by Bajoria (2025) and Mishra and Sato (2025), the researchers can justify the use of mathematical proofs over simple database logs. These studies provide the technical validation needed to claim that a centralized, LAN-based system can achieve "blockchain-level" security without the need for decentralized consensus.

### **2.2.2 Local Studies and Systems**

In the Philippines, the development of document tracking systems is heavily influenced by the legal mandates of the Ease of Doing Business and Efficient Government Service Delivery Act (Republic Act No. 11032). Local research focuses on the practical application of synchronization technologies, such as QR codes and barcodes, to improve administrative transparency and accountability within government units and higher education institutions (Acedo, 2025; Piloton et al., 2023).

The synthesis of local studies reveals a strong emphasis on user acceptability and the digitization of multi-stage workflows. Acedo (2025) and Farin (2024) have proven that QR-based tracking is highly accepted in the Philippine academic and government sectors due to its simplicity and effectiveness in providing verifiable timestamps. Other researchers, such as Acoba (2024), Raralio et al. (2025), and the developers of DocTrack (2022), have focused on specialized environments like judicial courts and procurement offices, demonstrating the utility of central repositories for case management and purchase requests (Rellon et al., 2020). Furthermore, studies by Trinidad (2022) and Olalia and Bugayong (2014) highlight the importance of digital repositories for institutional accreditation, where the management of thousands of exhibits is a critical requirement (Szkiel, 2024; Giraldo, 2016). Despite these successes, local systems often rely on database-level logs that remain vulnerable to "silent tampering" by administrators.

These local studies help the making of this thesis by identifying the specific operational constraints and user preferences within the Philippine bureaucracy. The high acceptability of QR codes documented by Farin (2024) and Piloton et al. (2023) justifies their use as the primary synchronization mechanism in the DTS prototype. By analyzing the workflow gaps in existing systems like CatSU's DTS (Acedo, 2025) and J-CIMS (Acoba, 2024), the researchers can focus on the unique contribution of this project: adding a layer of cryptographic non-repudiation that these local systems currently lack. This ensures that the prototype is not only compliant with RA 11032 but also addresses the data integrity risks identified in the local literature.

## **2.3 Synthesis of the Review**

The synthesis of the reviewed literature and studies establishes a clear technical progression from manual, paper-based tracking to basic digital repositories. The literature consistently identifies the inherent failures of manual logbooks, such as systemic misplacement (Rokosu, 2025), information opacity (Bala, 2020), and legal non-compliance with turnaround mandates (Piloton, 2023; RA 11032, 2018). Foreign studies on high-integrity judicial systems (Saman & Haider, 2012; Electronic Judiciary in Indonesia, 2021) and the U.S. Federal Courts (Greenwood, 2012) demonstrate that while centralized digitization significantly improves case throughput, it remains vulnerable to data corruption and unauthorized modifications without advanced authentication layers (Al-khafaji et al., 2020; Zhai et al., 2022).

The transition toward secure architectures is characterized by the adoption of mathematical-based security measures that ensure non-repudiation and tamper-evidence. Scholarly works on hash-chaining (Crosby & Wallach, 2009; Kim & Kim, 2024) and digital signatures (Bernstein et al., 2012; Chavarro et al., 2018) prove that security must be embedded directly into the data structure rather than relying on supervisor oversight. Furthermore, the integration of synchronization technologies like QR codes (Acedo, 2025; Farin, 2024) and NFC/IoT (Abidin et al., 2019, 2020) provides the necessary bridge between physical file movement and digital records. This ensures that the digital trail accurately reflects the ground truth of administrative actions, fulfilling the accountability requirements of modern governance (Acedo, 2025; Singh, 2024).

From a socio-technical perspective, the success of these systems depends on competing with the perceived convenience of "Shadow IT" and unofficial workflows. Research into user behavior identifies shadow systems, such as Google Sheets, as rational coping mechanisms for rigid IT environments (Behrens, 2009; Rentrop & Zimmermann, 2020). However, the literature warns that these unapproved tools introduce severe security risks and fragmented databases (Haag & Eckhardt, 2024; Marchewitz, 2026). Consequently, for a DTS to be effective, it must integrate human-centric design principles (Aqeel et al., 2023; Izzulhaq et al., 2022) and decision-support algorithms (Keen & Scott Morton, 1978; Ngan et al., 2022) to provide a user experience that is both more secure and faster than the informal alternatives (Towards a Bespoke DTS, 2019).

Despite these advancements, a critical research gap remains in the implementation of mathematical-based security within local, resource-constrained government environments. Most existing local systems in the Philippines rely on "Policy-Based Security," where the integrity of document logs depends on the hope that administrators will follow rules rather than on mathematically unbreakable proofs (Bajoria, 2025; Chavarro et al., 2018). While international studies propose decentralized blockchain solutions to solve this, these architectures are often too computationally expensive and complex for the local area networks (LAN) typical of provincial government offices (Mishra & Sato, 2025). Furthermore, current local prototypes often lack a unified intake protocol that enforces a single primary key for every document from intake to release (Acedo, 2025; Piloton, 2023).

This study addresses these gaps by implementing a high-security "Trust Builder" prototype that provides blockchain-level integrity within a centralized, LAN-based environment. By integrating SHA-256 hash-chaining (Crosby & Wallach, 2009) and Ed25519 digital signatures (Bernstein et al., 2012), the proposed system shifts the source of trust from human supervisors to verifiable mathematical proofs. The system’s "Active Guard" and "Auto-Freeze" mechanisms provide a real-time defense against the "silent tampering" identified in the literature (Zhai et al., 2022), while its smart routing and QR-sync features ensure it matches the speed of informal shadow systems (Behrens, 2009). This provides DepEd Iligan City with a specialized security artifact that enforces institutional accountability and prevents the proliferation of unofficial tracking methods.

## **2.4 Theoretical Framework**

The development of the Document Tracking System (DTS) prototype is grounded in a multi-disciplinary theoretical framework that bridges the gap between administrative case management and cryptographic engineering. These theories serve as the "Knowledge Base" for the Design Science Research (DSR) methodology, providing the necessary rigor to justify the architectural decisions behind the system's core modules (Hevner, 2007). By anchoring the artifact in established theories, the research ensures that the "Trust Builder" and "Active Guard" mechanisms are not merely technical features but are evidence-based solutions to identified institutional problems.

The primary foundation of the system is **Information Integrity Theory**, which focuses on maintaining the accuracy, consistency, and reliability of data over its entire lifecycle. This theory provides the justification for implementing "Mathematical-Based Security" through SHA-256 hash-chaining and Ed25519 digital signatures (Bajoria, 2025; Bernstein et al., 2012). It shifts the source of institutional trust from policy-based human oversight to verifiable mathematical proofs, ensuring that document logs are tamper-evident and non-repudiable. Furthermore, **Case Management Theory** (Frankel et al., 2018) provides the structural logic for monitoring the document through its "Intake," "Processing," and "Releasing" stages. This theoretical lens allows the system to accurately digitize the physical movement of correspondence while maintaining a strict audit trail of departmental responsibility.

To address the operational challenges of high-volume routing and physical synchronization, the study incorporates **Decision Support Theory** (Keen & Scott Morton, 1978) and **General System Theory** (Bertalanffy, 1968). Decision Support Theory informs the development of the AI-assisted route prediction module, which utilizes learned keyword weights to help Records Officers manage complex routing tasks efficiently (Ngan et al., 2022). Meanwhile, General System Theory views the DepEd Division Office as a socio-technical system where technological tools must harmonize with human habits. This theory justifies the integration of QR code scanning during physical hand-offs, ensuring that the digital status updates are perfectly synchronized with the physical location of the files (Bertalanffy, 1968; Acedo, 2025). Together, these theories provide the comprehensive framework needed to design, implement, and evaluate the technical integrity of the DTS artifact.

## **2.5 Conceptual Framework**

The conceptual framework of this study is operationalized through an **Input-Process-Output (IPO) model**, which illustrates the logical flow and transformation of data within the DTS prototype. This model serves as the blueprint for translating the theoretical foundations of the study into a functional IT artifact. By mapping the system's technical requirements to its organizational outcomes, the conceptual framework provides a structured approach to solving the identified gaps in document accountability and data integrity.

-   **INPUT:** The system intake begins with the collection of document metadata (Title, Purpose, and Submitter Information), user authentication credentials, and security initialization data. For cryptographic operations, the system requires user-provided Security PINs to derive encryption keys. Additionally, the system inputs include departmental route definitions and real-time scanning data generated via QR codes during physical document transfers.
-   **PROCESS:** The central processing phase is managed by the **"Trust Builder"** module, which performs SHA-256 hash-chaining and Ed25519 signature generation to ensure mathematical non-repudiation. Simultaneously, the **"Active Guard"** middleware serves as a real-time integrity monitor, verifying document states before authorizing any movement. The system also incorporates **Decision Support logic** through TF-IDF-inspired route prediction and employs asynchronous background processing to handle high-volume analytics and system-wide integrity audits.
-   **OUTPUT:** The primary outputs of the DTS include a tamper-evident, immutable audit trail for every document and cryptographically signed logs that provide verifiable proof of administrative actions. The system also delivers real-time status visibility for both guests and personnel, automated integrity reports for system administrators, and bottleneck analytics. Collectively, these outputs ensure full compliance with the transparency and efficiency mandates of Republic Act No. 11032.
